# Percussion technique: how professional percussionists hold and move the mallets

This file is the target of the motion of the percussion players of the app. It describes the
grip of the mallets, the sticks, the beater, and the cymbals, their directions between strikes and
at a strike, the stroke, and the playing spot of professional percussionists, with a source for
each fact. Sections 1 to 4 give the timpani, and sections 5 to 9 the instruments of the percussion
section.

**Upkeep.** This file is current. It is not a record of one date, as a research report is. A
round that changes the percussion motion of the app updates this file in the same commit: section
10 "Use in the app", and each fact that the round finds wrong. Each new fact gets its source. The
decision file [`2026-10-19-elephant-grip.md`](../decisions/2026-10-19-elephant-grip.md) records
this rule. The research report
[`2026-10-19-elephant-grip.md`](../research/2026-10-19-elephant-grip.md) holds the measures of the
app.

**Source marks.** Each fact names its source by a number of section 11. "(abstract)" marks a fact
from an abstract or a search summary, because the full text did not open. "(secondary)" marks a
fact that another source quotes. "(derived)" marks a value that this file calculates from the
values of its sources. A fact with no mark comes from the text of the source.

## Terms

- **Fulcrum.** The point where the hand pinches the shaft, between the thumb and a finger. The
  mallet turns around it.
- **Butt end.** The end of the shaft opposite to the head.
- **Playing spot.** The place on the head where the mallet strikes.
- **V.** The angle between the two mallets, seen from above.

## 1. The grip

- The fulcrum is between the thumb and the index finger. The back fingers wrap the shaft
  loosely [1]. Wikipedia puts the fulcrum between the thumb and the middle finger [8
  (secondary)].
- The thumb lies flat along the shaft, and its nail points to the head [1]. In the French grip,
  the thumb points down the shaft toward the head [3]. Thus the head leaves the front of the fist,
  at the side of the thumb and the index finger, and the shaft lies along the thumb side of the
  hand (derived).
- The butt end sticks out about 0.5 to 1 inch (13 to 25 mm) behind the hand [1]. It stays loose
  in the palm, with some play between the butt and the hand [6].
- The fulcrum is about one third of the way up the shaft [12 (abstract)].

## 2. The three grips

| Grip | The hands | The stroke | The V between the mallets |
|---|---|---|---|
| German | The backs of the hands up, the thumbs in, toward each other [1] | From the wrist [1] | About 90° in front of the player [1] |
| French | The thumbs up [1] [3] | Mostly a turn of the forearm, as a turn of a door knob [1] [3] | About 20° to 30° [1]. The mallets are almost parallel [4] |
| American | Between the two: the thumbs and the palms at about 45° [1] [2] | A mix | Between 20° and 90° [1] |

Thus each mallet points forward and in, 10° to 45° from the straight forward direction (derived
from the V).

## 3. The mallet between strikes and at a strike

- The shaft is 10° to 25° from the drum head [3]. Peters gives about 10° above the head [2].
- The mallets do not go below the level [5].
- No source gives the height of the head between strikes. The heights of 12 inches [5] and 18
  inches [7] are drills.
- The upper arm hangs beside the body, and the elbow bends so that the mallet reaches the head
  [3]. The shoulders do not lift [1].
- The wrist has a small bend, which lets it turn [4].
- A timpanist can stand to play [11], or sit on a stool [4]. A stool can also be only a support to
  lean against [3].

## 4. The stroke and the playing spot

- The mallet lifts out of the head at once, because a long contact damps the tone [1]. The
  upstroke starts before the strike, as if the player pulls the sound out of the drum [3]. The
  stroke is a toss with a natural rebound [5].
- The stroke turns the wrist, and the arm moves little [4] [10]. The arm moves more in loud
  play [10].
- The playing spot is about 2 to 4 inches (5 to 10 cm) in from the rim [2] [5], or one third of
  the way from the rim to the middle, about 4 inches [3] [9]. A spot 6 inches or more in gives a
  dead tone [5].
- The spot is directly in front of the player [7]. The smallest drum plays nearer the rim, at
  about 2 inches [7].
- The two mallets strike about 6 inches (15 cm) apart [4], in the same area of the head [7].

## 5. The bass drum

- The player stands behind the drum, with the left foot between the legs of the stand, so that
  one hand reaches both heads and the beater stays parallel to the head [13].
- The right hand holds one beater, about one third up from the butt, loosely, with the thumb on
  the side, as in the legato snare grip [13] [14].
- The playing spot is off the center: halfway between the edge and the center [15], one third in
  from the rim [16], or just below the center, in front of the knee of the player [14].
- The stroke uses the wrist and the forearm, with an upstroke, and the upper arm for loud notes
  [15] [16]. The beater rebounds and moves away, so that the head rings [13].
- The free hand muffles the head with the open palm and the fingers, mostly in the rests [13]
  [14]. A single note often needs no muffling [13].
- The frame tilts the drum from vertical to horizontal. Rolls take a tilt of about 45°, but the
  drum does not lie flat [14] [28].

## 6. The snare drum

- The head is about 3 to 4 inches (a hand span) below the navel [17]. The drum stands level [17].
- The fulcrum is one third up from the butt, between the thumb and the forefinger. The back
  fingers wrap the stick loosely, and about two thirds of the stick leaves the front of the hand
  [17] [18].
- The palms are nearly flat, and the tips of the sticks make a V of 60° to 90° [17] [18] [19].
- The wrists are about 1 inch above the head, so the sticks are almost parallel to the floor, and
  slant down a little at a strike [17].
- The sticks strike near the middle of the head, about 1 inch above it on the line out from the
  body [18], or a little off the middle [9].
- The elbows are about a fist from the body, the upper arms hang mostly still, and the wrist
  makes the stroke [17]. A full stroke lifts the tip about 8 inches [18].

## 7. The crash cymbals and the suspended cymbal

- Hand cymbals of all weights are 14 to 22 inches wide [23]: 0.356 to 0.559 m (derived). The
  smallest real hand cymbal of these sources is thus 14 inches, 0.356 m.
- The thumb and the forefinger hold the strap near the bell. The hand does not go through the
  strap [15] [20] [23].
- The plates are in front of the middle of the chest, with their edges about 3 inches from the
  body [21], or with the top edges at about eye level [15] [20].
- The plates are not vertical. The left plate can lie almost level under the left hand, with the
  elbow near the stomach, and the right plate comes in at about 20° to it [20]. Other values are
  about 45°, with the plate of the strong hand on top [23], and 5° to 45° from vertical [16].
- The right plate leads and comes down onto the left plate, one edge first, as a flam [20] [21]
  [23]. Better players move both plates [20] [22].
- After a long crash, the plates keep moving and turn out to let the sound go to the hall [21],
  or go up above the shoulders [22]. A short note stops against the chest or the stomach [15]
  [20] [22]. No concert source gives the rest place. A marching method holds the plates at the
  sides, almost vertical [24].
- A part marked "with stick" or "with mallet" plays on a suspended cymbal: one cymbal on a stand,
  struck with a stick or a beater, not with another cymbal [23] [32].
- A suspended cymbal roll uses medium to soft yarn mallets. A cymbal of 17 inches or less takes a
  lighter mallet [30]. Suspended cymbals for orchestras and bands are sold at 18 inches
  [33 (abstract)].
- The roll plays near the edge, about half an inch in, with the left and the right mallets
  opposite each other [21] [30] [32]. Another source tells the player not to play exactly
  opposite, because the plate then rocks [31].
- The stroke is legato, as on a marimba bar, and comes off the cymbal at the end of the roll
  [30]. Single notes play at the very edge with a legato stroke [31].
- No source of this file gives the height of the stand.

## 8. The triangle and the tambourine

- The left hand holds the triangle by a clip with a loop, at eye level, and the right hand holds
  the beater [15] [25].
- The beater strikes the middle of the bottom side [9], or the outside near the bottom [16], with
  a motion that pushes the bottom side away [15]. A strike at 45° gives a darker sound [9].
- The fingers of the left hand mute the triangle [16] [25].
- The left hand holds the tambourine by its frame, at or a little below the chest, tilted in at
  about 45°, and the right hand strikes it [15] [16]. No source of this file gives a tambourine on
  a stand.

## 9. The xylophone

- The player stands with the feet about the width of the shoulders apart, and moves the body from
  side to side behind the notes, with few steps [26] [27].
- The bars are at the height where the wrist bends naturally, as for a snare drum. The forearms
  are a little below parallel to the floor [26] [27].
- The grip is the matched grip of the snare drum [18] [26]. The mallets make a symmetrical V, and
  do not cross [27]. No source gives the angle.
- A mallet strikes a natural bar at its middle, and a sharp bar at its edge [9], the edge near the
  player (search summary of [29]).
- The wrists stay low, and the elbows move forward to reach the back row [27].
- A scale plays with alternate strokes, from either hand, and the hands also alternate on the
  sharp bars of a chromatic scale [34]. Mallet technique rests on alternate single strokes. Where
  full alternation would make the hands cross, the player puts in double strokes, two strokes of
  one hand [35].

## 10. Use in the app

The elephant plays the timpani of the app (`PERCUSSION_HOLDS.timpani` of
`src/pose/percussion-rig.ts`, `TIMPANI` of `src/models/instruments/percussion.ts`). Each item
gives the rule of the app, or where the app differs from sections 1 to 4. A round that changes the
motion updates the items.

- **The grip.** The head of each mallet leaves the front of the fist, and the shaft lies along
  the thumb, near the line of the forearm (`TIMPANI.malletTilt`), as section 1 gives. The butt end
  stays in the paw.
- **The direction between strikes.** Each mallet points forward and in, 14° to 16° from forward
  (a V of about 30°, as the French and the American grips), and 1° to 7° above the level
  (`PercussionHold.across`, sections 2 and 3). A wider V of the German grip turns the paw of the
  elephant further on its forearm (`docs/research/2026-10-19-elephant-grip.md`). At a strike,
  each mallet points forward and 20° to 23° down (section 3).
- **The elbows.** Each upper arm hangs near the side of the body, and the elbow points down and
  a little out (`PercussionHold.poleL` and `poleR`), as section 3 gives. Section 3 gives no
  distance of the elbow from the body, and section 6 gives about a fist for the snare drum. With
  the elbows farther
  out on the old arms, the user saw "an unnatural gap between the arm and the body" from the
  back (`docs/decisions/2026-10-22-elephant-crease.md`). The quad arm tubes show no gap from the
  back with the elbows a little out, and the thick upper arms go less far into the round belly
  (`docs/decisions/2026-10-25-elephant-hippo-creases.md`).
- **The stroke.** The head lifts before the note, comes down at its full speed at the note
  start, and rebounds (`STRIKE_MOTION` of `src/pose/strikes.ts`), as section 4 gives.
- **The playing spot.** The app differs. Each mallet strikes 0.25 m in from the near rim
  (`PercussionHold.rim`), because the short arms of the elephant hold the paws above the near
  rims, as the user chose (`docs/decisions/2026-10-19-elephant-pose.md`). A real player strikes
  5 to 10 cm in (section 4). Each hand plays only the front kettle on its side of the trunk
  (`PercussionHold.pair`), so the two mallets never strike one head together.

The badger plays the other percussion instruments (`PERCUSSION_HOLDS` of
`src/pose/percussion-rig.ts`, `BASS_DRUM`, `SNARE_DRUM`, `TAMBOURINE`, `TRIANGLE`, and
`XYLOPHONE` of `src/models/instruments/percussion.ts`). Its short arms reach 0.27 m from the
shoulder joint to the palm (`docs/decisions/2026-10-20-mesh-badger.md`).

- **The bass drum.** The badger stands behind the drum at its front left, and the beater in the
  right paw strikes 12 cm below the middle of the head, as section 5 gives. The left arm hangs,
  because a single note often needs no muffling (section 5). The beater leaves the side of the
  fist: no source gives where it leaves the fist.
- **The snare drum.** The head stands 0.46 m high, at about the height of the wrists, and 0.45 m in
  front. The sticks leave the front of the fists and make a V of about 66°, 9° down at a strike,
  as section 6 gives. Between strikes, each stick keeps its strike direction seen from above,
  and the wrist lifts the tip about 17 cm above its spot (`MalletAcross.pitch`). The tip stays
  over the head, about 4 cm from its spot, and the V opens to about 78°. Section 6 gives a lift
  of about 8 inches (20 cm) for a full stroke. The elbows point down and out.
- **The triangle and the tambourine.** The left paw holds the triangle by its clip at the height
  of the mouth of the badger, below the eye level of section 8, because the short arm reaches no
  higher in front of the long snout. The clip lies in the palm under the curled fingers and
  leaves the heel of the paw, and the triangle hangs from it in a loop of cord, as section 8 gives
  (`TRIANGLE_CLIP` of `src/models/instruments/percussion.ts`). Before 2026-10-25, a thin cord of
  1.5 mm joined the heel of the paw to the triangle, and the triangle seemed to float below the
  paw. The paw of the app stands with the fingers up, and a real player holds the clip between
  the thumb and the fingers at eye level: no source of section 11 gives the turn of the hand. The beater in the right paw strikes the triangle, and the
  tambourine stands on a stand at the front right, where section 8 gives a tambourine in the hand.
- **The cymbals.** The badger plays a suspended cymbal, as section 7 gives for a part "with
  mallet": its short arms cannot hold hand cymbals of the smallest real size (14 inches) out of its
  round body (`docs/decisions/2026-10-20-badger-cymbals.md`). The cymbal is 0.40 m wide (about 16
  inches), level on a stand. The yarn mallets take the grip of the snare sticks, and the paws
  strike in turn about half an inch in from the edge, opposite each other, as section 7 gives.
  Between strikes, each mallet keeps its strike direction seen from above, and the wrist lifts
  the head about 12 cm above its spot (`MalletAcross.pitch`). The head stays over the plate,
  about 3 cm in from the rim. No source of section 11 gives the height between strikes. The
  roll of the app is the alternate strikes of the notes. The app has no muffling of the plate. The
  hand cymbals of the app meet in front of the shoulders, vertical (`PERCUSSION_PLACES`).
- **The xylophone.** The mallets leave the front of the fists, as the sticks of the snare drum,
  as section 9 gives. The badger moves the body from side to side behind the notes with the feet
  in place, as section 9 gives with few steps: the hips move up to 2 cm, and the spine leans up
  to 10° toward the mean place of the notes, in the 0.6 s before each note
  (`PercussionHold.side`). The short legs of the badger tilt far with a small move of the hips,
  so the spine takes most of the move. The neck and the head turn back by the side lean, so the
  eyes stay level and look at the bars. The app differs: the spine also leans 11° forward over the
  bars (`PercussionHold.lean`), because the short arms do not reach the bars from an upright
  body. Section 9 gives no lean. The app also differs in the size of the bars: they are 3 cm
  wide and 12 cm to 8 cm long, and the near edge of the bars stands 0.42 m in front of the
  badger (`XYLOPHONE`), because the short arms reach the bars only in a band about 0.12 m deep.
  With bars of a real size, the sharp bars stood about 11 cm out of reach. A mallet strikes a
  sharp bar 2 cm from its edge near the player, as section 9 gives. A part below or
  above the bars plays in the octave that puts its highest key in the highest octave of the
  bars (`xylophoneShift()` of `src/pose/percussion-rig.ts`), where the short arms reach best. No
  source of section 11 gives the octave that a player takes for such a part. The mallets make a V
  and do not cross, as section 9 gives, but the app differs in three rules. A repeat on one
  bar, or a strike within 2 cm of the last bar of the other hand, stays in one hand
  (`PercussionHold.gap`). A run stays in one hand until it leaves the reach of the paw, 0.53 m
  from the shoulder joint on the floor plane, and then goes on in the other paw
  (`PercussionHold.reach`): the app plays the double strokes of section 9 in place of the
  alternate strokes of a scale. With full alternation, each head passes the other head at about
  the same height, and the shafts touched in 4% to 6% of the samples of the test score (open).
  The left mallet strikes 2 cm nearer to the player than the middle of a bar, the right mallet
  2 cm farther (`PERCUSSION_HOLDS.xylophone`). Between strikes, each mallet
  keeps its strike direction seen from above, and the wrist lifts the head about 13 cm above its
  bar (`MalletAcross.pitch`), so the two heads keep at least 1.6 cm apart. No source of section 11
  gives the height between strikes.
