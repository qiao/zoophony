# Wind technique: how professional wind and brass players hold and move their instruments

This file is the target of the motion of the wind and brass players of the app: the flute, the
oboe, the clarinet, the bassoon, the horn, the trumpet, the trombone, and the tuba. It describes
the hold, the angles, the hands, the trombone slide, the breath, the rest pose, and the seated
posture of professional players, with a source for each fact. The trombone has the most detail,
because its slide moves the most.

**Upkeep.** This file is current. A change of the wind motion of the app updates section 4 "Use
in the app" and each fact that it finds wrong, in the same commit. Each new fact gets its source.

**Source marks.** Each fact names its source by a number of section 5. "(abstract)" marks a fact
from an abstract or a search summary, because the full text did not open. "(secondary)" marks a
fact that another source quotes. "(derived)" marks a value that this file calculates from the
values of its sources. A fact with no mark comes from the text of the source.

## Terms

- **Position.** The place of the trombone slide. The first position is the closed slide. Each
  position further out lowers the pitch by one semitone, to the seventh position [1] [8].
- **Partial.** One note of the harmonic series of the tube. A brass player moves from one partial
  to the next with the lips and the air, with no change of the slide or the valves [8].
- **Alternate position.** A second position for the same note, on a higher partial [8].
- **Slide brace.** The cross bar of the moving slide. The right hand holds it [1].
- **Slide lock.** The lock near the mouthpiece that holds the slide closed [1].
- **Legato tongue.** A light stroke of the tongue, as the syllable "lah" or "dah", that divides
  two slurred notes while the slide moves [1] [12].
- **Natural slur.** A slur where the slide moves against the direction of the pitch. It needs no
  tongue [12].
- **Rest pose.** The pose of a player while the instrument is off the mouth.
- **Ready pose.** The pose of a player just before the instrument goes to the mouth.

## 1. Trombone

### 1.1 The hold

- **Weight.** The left hand holds all the weight of the trombone. The right arm holds no weight
  and only moves the slide [1] [2] [3].
- **Left hand.** The thumb and the index finger make an "L" [1] [2] [5]. The thumb wraps around
  the bell brace, the index finger lies along the mouthpiece brace or the leadpipe and points to
  the mouthpiece, and the other three fingers wrap around the slide tubes between the mouthpiece
  brace and the slide brace [1] [2] [5].
- **Left arm.** The left forearm is "a pillar of support", near the vertical, with no "chicken
  wing" elbow [4]. The bell does not rest on the left shoulder, and the left arm holds the weight
  [1] [5]. One source puts the instrument on the left shoulder [7].
- **Right hand.** The tips of the thumb, the index finger, and the middle finger hold the slide
  brace, and the other two fingers curl into the palm with no contact [1] [3]. The grip is light,
  and the fingers turn around the brace as the wrist makes small adjustments [1]. The thumb stays
  on the brace, with "NO throw and catch" [3]. The wrists stay "straight and in line with your
  arms" [2]. The right hand does not touch the bell [3].
- **Right thumb and fingers on the brace.** The thumb is on the top of the brace and the fingers
  on the bottom [2] [4]. One teacher pinches the brace "at the bottom corner", with the other
  fingers below the slide [64]. The ring finger and the little finger go beneath the bottom slide
  tube [61]. Thus the thumb points up the brace, on its side near the player (derived).
- **Right palm.** The sources disagree. The palm faces the floor [61], or the chest [62]. Burtis
  gives a range from the wrist "sideways in relation to the face" to "the bottom of the wrist"
  toward the player [63]. At the seventh position, the palm faces the slide tube, "as if you were
  reaching out a long way to shake someone's hand" [61]. No source of this file puts the palm
  toward the audience or the thumb under the brace.
- **The bell.** The angle between the slide and the bell section is a little less than 90° [1]
  [6].
- **The angle.** The trombone points toward the audience, and never down toward the ground [4].
  A bell-front brass instrument does not go above the level, because that posture adds tension
  [1]. No source of this file gives the angle of the slide in degrees.
- **The head and the torso.** The back stays straight and the head up. A slump to the left with
  the torso or the neck limits the air and the slide [1]. The eyes look straight forward [4].
- **The stand.** The slide and the bell stay at the left of the music stand and out from under
  it, so that the player sees the conductor [4].
- **The side.** The player stands "next to" the trombone, so that the joint of the collarbone and
  the breastbone can help the slide arm [3].

### 1.2 The slide positions

The tube of a B♭ tenor trombone is 9 ft (2.74 m) with the slide closed [8]. The slide is a U, so
a move of the slide by d adds 2d of tube. Thus position n is `2.74 m / 2 × (2^((n−1)/12) − 1)`
from the first position (derived).

| Position | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance from position 1 (derived) | 0 | 8.2 cm | 16.8 cm | 26.0 cm | 35.7 cm | 45.9 cm | 56.8 cm |
| Step from the position before (derived) | | 8.2 cm | 8.6 cm | 9.2 cm | 9.7 cm | 10.3 cm | 10.9 cm |

- **Steps.** The step grows from 3 in (7.6 cm) between the first and the second position to just
  over 4 in (10.2 cm) between the sixth and the seventh [1]. Another source gives about 3¼ in
  (8 cm) for each step [8].
- **Landmarks.** The third position is about at the rim of the bell, and the fourth position is
  "about 10 cm lower" [7].
- **Measures.** The fifth position extends the slide "by around 0.4 m" [11]. Three bass
  trombonists moved the arm "ca. 38 cm" from the first to the fifth position, and "ca. 20 cm"
  from the third to the first position [9]. The slide moves "approximately two feet" (61 cm) from
  the first to the seventh position [18].
- **Tuning.** Each position moves a little with the partial [1]. The fifth partial is flat on
  most trombones and needs a slide a little shorter. The seventh partial (A♭4 in the first
  position) is "nearly always 31 cents" flat, and the player corrects it with the slide or with an
  alternate position [8].

### 1.3 Alternate positions

- B♭3 plays in the first or the fifth position. F4 plays in the first, the fourth, or the sixth
  position [8].
- Trombonists use alternate positions often, to move the slide less in fast passages, and for a
  glissando on one partial. For high notes, the player needs only the first four positions [8].
- One teacher uses alternates "primarily to create or avoid slurs", and not for the direction or
  the distance of the slide [3]. An alternate position that moves the slide against the pitch
  gives a natural slur [12].
- The F attachment, a valve that the left thumb works, gives alternate positions for the long
  positions [1] [8].
- **The seventh partial (derived).** A player who does not use the flat seventh partial plays
  A♭4 in the third position, G4 in the fourth position, and F♯4 in the fifth position, from the
  eighth partial (B♭4).

### 1.4 The arm for each position

- **The joints.** Four joints move the slide: the wrist, the elbow, the shoulder, and the joint
  of the collarbone and the breastbone [3].
- **The shares.** "The wrist leads the movement for the slide", and a larger move adds the elbow.
  The shoulder moves very little, only at the sixth and the seventh positions [1]. Another
  teacher puts "Most of the work" in the elbow and the shoulder, with the wrist "like a hinge that
  snaps the slide into position" [3].
- **The far positions.** The outer positions need long arms, and grip extenders exist for short
  arms [1] [7]. At the seventh position, the player can change the thumb for the ring finger on
  the brace [3]. Thus the arm is at its full reach at the seventh position.
- **The path.** The slide moves straight in and out, "with NO CURVES" [3].
- **Measured angles.** In two motion capture tests of one standing trombonist, from the first to
  the sixth position, the right elbow flexion went from 114° to 28° and from 125° to 17°. The
  right shoulder flexion went from 16° to 45° and from 35° to 70°. The left elbow stayed between
  120° and 131°. The turn of the right wrist had the largest range, 133°. 0° is the straight arm
  of the anatomical position [18].
- **The left side is still.** The left arm takes "primarily static loading", and the right arm
  "dynamic loading" [18].
- **The head and the trombone.** In seven players, the neck flexion varied with the downward
  angle of the instrument, and the players tend "to hold the instrument straight forward" [18].

### 1.5 The slide move

- **Fast.** The slide moves fast between the positions, so that no glissando sounds between the
  notes [1]. The right arm is "quick and precise, yet relaxed" [3]. The slide moves as quickly
  from the first to the sixth position as from the first to the second [12].
- **Late.** "Strive to move the slide as late as possible!" [13]. The slide moves at the start of
  the next note [3], and keeps each position "for the longest possible time, even with staccato
  notes" [14].
- **The stop.** The slide stops in the position of each note. Only extremely fast staccato
  passages are an exception [3].
- **Legato.** When the slide and the pitch move in the same direction, the player tongues
  lightly, so that no smear sounds [1] [12]. One trombonist warns against a fast "flick" of the
  slide for each slur, with the air "with (or preferably ahead) of" the slide [15].
- **The time of a legato change.** Three professional bass trombonists played legato changes from
  the first to the fifth position. The change of the sound took 98 ms on the mean, and 60 ms with
  the second valve in place of the slide [9].
- **Speed.** The slide speed sometimes exceeds 2 m/s, and the peak speed is roughly proportional
  to the distance [10 (secondary)].

### 1.6 The breath

- **The mouth.** Brass players breathe in through the mouth [1] [6].
- **What moves.** The rib cage expands across its width, and the expansion goes down into the
  lower torso. The shoulders and the arms do not move in a deep breath. A student who raises the
  shoulders breathes shallowly [1].
- **The order.** Before a long note, the thorax and the abdomen expand, and the abdomen reaches
  its maximum expansion first [16].
- **Standing and sitting.** In 113 woodwind and brass players, the chest expanded more when they
  stood, and the abdomen less than in each seated posture [17].
- **The time.** A breathing exercise shortens the breath in from 4 counts to 1 count [1]. In a
  medium 4/4 tempo, the player breathes in on beat four, over beats three and four in a fast
  tempo, and on the upbeat after beat four in a very slow tempo [24]. Reed players (oboe,
  bassoon, clarinet, and alto saxophone) took breaths of "typically about 300 ms" [38]. A breath
  of one beat at 100 to 150 bpm takes 0.4 s to 0.6 s (derived).

### 1.7 The rest and the ready pose

- **The slide lock.** The slide lock is always on when the slide is not in use [1]. The set-up of
  a lesson starts with the slide lock engaged [5].
- **On the floor.** A trombone on the floor rests on the bell, the mouthpiece or the leadpipe, and
  the tuning slide, and never on the slide [6].
- No source of this file gives where an orchestral trombonist holds the trombone between two
  entrances, or how long before an entrance the player lifts it.

### 1.8 The seated posture of a brass player

- The player sits balanced and upright, off the back of the chair, with the feet flat on the
  floor [4].
- The torso stays symmetrical, with no twist, and the shoulders stay over the hips [1].
- Brass players sit "as if you are standing", or on the front edge of a flat seat, with the trunk
  erect and the thighs down [16].
- The abdominal muscles work less on a flat or a downward sloping seat than in standing [16]. In
  [17], they worked at about 2/3 of the level of standing, and the players preferred to stand.
- The arms stay away from the rib cage, so that the rib cage can expand [1].

## 2. Trumpet, horn, oboe, and flute

### 2.1 Trumpet

- **The hold.** The left hand holds the weight [1] [21]. It holds the valve casing, with the ring
  finger (or the middle finger) in the ring of the third valve slide, and the thumb in the saddle
  of the first valve slide [1] [19] [20]. The valve casing stays vertical [21], with the right
  hand vertical and the left hand horizontal [20].
- **The right hand.** The index, the middle, and the ring finger rest on the valves 1, 2, and 3,
  with the fingertips and arched fingers [1] [20]. The sources disagree on the right thumb: under
  the leadpipe between the first and the second valve casing [1] [19] [20], or "ON the first
  valve" [21]. The little finger stays on top of the finger hook or floats free [1] [19] [20].
- **The angles.** The trumpet is "angled slightly down" [19]. The angle of the trumpet to the
  plane of the face is "slightly less than 90 degrees", and the elbows are at about 45° to the
  ground [22]. A bell above the level is a fault of an exaggerated posture [1].
- **The fingering.** The written D4 and C♯4 use the valves 1-3 and 1-2-3, the written E♭4 uses
  2-3, and the written A5 uses 1-2 [1].
- **The breath.** The shoulders stay down [22] [39]. The player breathes in through one corner of
  the mouth, with the mouthpiece on the lips [23].
- **The rest.** No source of this file gives the rest of an orchestral trumpet player.

### 2.2 Horn

- **The left hand.** The left hand holds the weight and works the valves. It has the shape of a C,
  with the little finger in the finger hook, the index, the middle, and the ring finger on the
  valve levers, and the thumb on the B♭ valve of a double horn [1] [25]. The left hand is at the
  middle of the body and below the face, and the horn goes from the middle of the body to the
  right side [29]. Tuckwell keeps the elbows near the body [28 (secondary)].
- **The right hand.** The right hand goes into the bell, relaxed, with the fingers together, "as
  though it were to be used to cup water". It rests against the bell wall at about 2 o'clock
  [1] [29]. Farkas holds the hand vertical, with the backs of the fingers on the far side of the
  bell and the thumb against the top of the throat [26 (secondary)]. The weight of the bell rests
  on the knuckles of the thumb and the index finger [1] [25].
- **The angles.** The horn lies at about 45° across the body, with the bell at the right of the
  torso and in front of it [25]. The mouthpiece slopes down [29]. The bell points past the right
  side of the body, so that the stomach does not mute it [1]. Tuckwell turns the head to the left
  to meet the mouthpiece [28 (secondary)].
- **The bell on the leg.** The sources disagree. Farkas sat with the lower rim of the bell on the
  outer side of the thigh [27 (secondary)]. Tuckwell holds the horn free of the body, also when
  seated [28 (secondary)]. The weight is on the hands, off the leg [1], and most players support
  the bell with the right arm [25].
- **The breath.** The sources disagree on the corners of the mouth: a wide smile (Farkas), "No
  smiling when inhaling!" (Wekre), or a jaw that drops off the mouthpiece [31 (secondary)].
- **The rest and the ready pose.** The horn rests on the lap, with the bell to the right [25],
  and the leadpipe and the mouthpiece up on the left side [30]. Then the players "raise their
  horns into playing position" [25]. No source gives the time of the lift.
- **The open notes.** The F horn is a tube of about 3.7 m (12 ft) [57]. Its fundamental is F1
  (MIDI 29), written C2, and it is "unplayable" on the F side [1] [56] [57]. Horn parts are
  written in F and sound a perfect fifth lower [56]. Thus the open notes are the written C3, G3,
  C4, E4, G4, and C5: the partials 2 to 6 and 8 [1] [57].
- **The fingering of the F horn.** The single F horn chart of Weidner lowers an open note by 1 to
  6 semitones with the valves 2, 1, 1-2, 2-3, 1-3, and 1-2-3 [55]. Up to the written C5, each note
  takes the nearest open note at or above it. Above the written C5, the chart takes only the 10th,
  12th, and 16th partials (written E5, G5, and C6). It plays C♯5 with 1-2, D5 with 1, E♭5 with 2,
  F5 with 1, F♯5 with 2, A♭5 with 2-3, A5 with 1-2, B♭5 with 1, and B5 with 2 [55].
- **The partials out of tune.** On the F horn, the 7th, 11th, 13th, and 14th partials (written
  B♭4, F♯5, between A♭5 and A5, and B♭5) are more than 30 cents out of tune. The 9th partial (D5)
  is within 5 cents, and the 15th (B5) is less than 16 cents flat [57]. On the natural horn, the
  7th, 11th, and 13th partials "sound quite flat" [56 (secondary)].
- **The double horn.** "All advanced players use the double horn" [56]. Its thumb valve changes
  the horn from F to B♭, and "pitches above g4 utilize the Bb trigger fingerings" [1].

### 2.3 Oboe

- **The hold.** The right thumb under the thumb rest carries the weight of the oboe [32] [34].
  The base of the thumbnail is under the thumb rest, and the left thumb touches the back of the
  oboe below the octave key [33]. One source shares the weight between the two thumbs and the
  embouchure [33].
- **The angle.** The oboe is at about 45° from the body [32] [34], or at about 40° [33]. Oboists
  outside the American school hold it "a few degrees closer to horizontal" [35]. The oboe comes to
  the mouth, and the head stays up [32] [34]. The oboe never rests on the knee in play [34].
- **The arms and the fingers.** The arms stay a little away from the sides, so that the rib cage
  can expand [33] [34]. The fingers curve in a C shape, and the pads of the fingers touch the keys
  [33]. Ray Still, former principal oboe of the Chicago Symphony, drops the wrists and keeps the
  arch of each finger the same when it opens and closes a key [37].
- **The elbows.** The arms hang loosely from the shoulders, and the elbows neither stick out nor
  stay close to the body [65]. The shoulders stay relaxed and low [66]. "The elbows and hands are
  free from the body" [33]. Thus the elbows hang below the shoulders, a little out from the sides
  (derived). No source gives the angle of the upper arm.
- **The breath.** The oboe needs little air, so an oboist breathes out the stale air before the
  next breath in [36] [39].
- **The rest.** In a long rest, the oboe lies across the legs with the tone holes up. In a short
  rest, the right hand holds it vertical on the right leg [33].

### 2.4 Flute

- **The hold.** Three points balance the flute: the lower lip and the chin, the base of the left
  index finger, and the right thumb [6] [44]. The left index finger is the fulcrum [41]. The
  sources disagree on the right thumb: Rockstro puts its tip on the inner side of the flute, to
  the right of the first finger [47 (secondary)], and Kincaid uses it to support the flute
  [46 (secondary)]. Only the fingers move [43] [46].
- **The right hand.** The right thumb is "directly under and in between the first and second
  fingers", and the right hand "forms a C" [46]. Thus the right hand comes to the flute from the
  side of the player, below and behind it, with the fingers on the keys on the top (derived).
- **The angles.** The flutist turns the head about 30° to the side, and the flute lies about
  square to the face [40]. The flute slopes down to the right by "approximately the width of the
  hand" from the level [45], and the head tilts toward the right ear [6]. A flute parallel to the
  floor with the elbows high is a fault [43].
- **The body.** The body faces 45° to the right, the player turns the head to the left, and the
  arms hang [43]. A seated player turns the chair a little to the right [46]. Seated, the elbows
  and the shoulders stay low [40].
- **The breath.** In four standing professional flutists, the inspiratory muscles held the rib
  cage at a high lung volume while they played [48 (abstract)]. Debost keeps the rib cage expanded
  when the note starts [41].
- **The rest.** The flute stands "vertically on the right leg with the right hand on the
  right-hand keys" [42].

## 3. Clarinet, bassoon, and tuba

- **Clarinet.** The clarinet is at the middle of the body, at about 45° from the body [49] [50]. It
  goes "from the mouth to between the knees" [6]. The right thumb under the thumb rest carries the
  weight, and the head does not tilt toward the clarinet [49] [50]. In a deep breath, the chest
  expands and the shoulders stay still [49]. In a rest, a seated player puts the right thumb on
  top of the thumb rest and the bell on the chair, on the seat between the legs, or on the knee.
  A standing player does not put the clarinet on the legs [60].
- **Bassoon.** The bassoon lies diagonal in front of the player and hangs on a seat strap, a neck
  strap, or a harness [52]. Most American bassoonists sit on a seat strap, with the bassoon on the
  right thigh [51]. The left hand is above the right hand, and the right hand rests against the
  crutch [52]. The bocal brings the reed to the mouth "at a nearly horizontal angle" [35], and the
  reed goes straight into the mouth [51]. The head and the torso stay straight and upright, and
  the player reads the music to the right side of the bell [51]. Thus the boot rests at the right
  thigh, and the bell rises at the left of the line of sight, at the height of the eyes or above
  (derived). The seat strap, the right thigh, and the base of the left index finger carry the
  bassoon, and the boot joint fits in the right palm [67]. A wet bassoon stands vertical during
  play [68]. No source of this file gives the rest between two entrances.
- **Tuba.** The tuba rests on the thigh, between the legs on the chair, or on a stand, and the
  player sits on the front of the chair with a stand [1] [53]. The right hand works the valves
  [54]. The left hand grips the tubing at the side of the tuba: on a rotary tuba "with her
  knuckles facing left", and on a piston tuba around the valve tubing "with his knuckles facing
  out" [53]. The bell is at the left of the head on a rotary tuba and at the right of the head on
  a piston tuba [53]. The player brings the tuba to the body, with no change of the back, the
  neck, or the head, and the tuba can be at a slight angle for the embouchure [1]. No source of
  this file gives the rest between two entrances.
- **Standing tuba.** A standing player carries the tuba on a strap that spreads the weight across
  both shoulders [58], or on a harness for "a more stable and upright playing position" [59]. Thus
  the hands of a standing player carry no weight, as in the seat (derived).

## 4. Use in the app

The holds are in `WIND_HOLDS` of `src/pose/winds.ts`, one for each instrument, with a hold of each
species that plays it (`PANDA_TROMBONE`, `FOX_FLUTE`, and the others). The notes, the slide, the
valves, and the breath come from `src/pose/wind-notes.ts`. The paws of the meshes are mittens with
one finger bone and a thumb with no bone, so no wind player shows single fingertips, and no thumb
reaches a key or a thumb rest.

These facts hold for each wind player:

- **The breath time (1.6).** The breath in takes `WIND_MOTION.inhaleSec`, and ends at the first
  note of a phrase after a gap of at least `PHRASE_GAP_SEC`.
- **The breath motion (1.6).** The app differs: the spine leans back at the top of the breath
  (`WIND_BODY.breath`), in place of an expansion of the chest and the abdomen.
- **The fingers of the woodwinds (2.3, 2.4).** Both paws close for a low note, and the right paw
  opens first up the octave (`holesClosed()`). The paws curl as one block (`WindHold.gripCurl`,
  `WIND_BODY.keyCurl`).
- **The lift (1.7, 2.2).** The winds lift first in the count-in (`REST_MOTION` of
  `src/pose/rest.ts`). No source gives the time.

### 4.1 The trombone of the panda

The panda plays with `PANDA_TROMBONE` and `PANDA_PLAY`. The trombone is `trombone()` of
`src/models/instruments/brass.ts`.

The app follows these facts:

- **The grips (1.1).** The left paw holds the weight, with the palm on the inner slide tubes near
  the mouthpiece brace (`faceTube`, `gripShiftL`). The right paw holds the low part of the upright
  slide brace from the right, with the palm to the left and a little toward the player, and the
  thumb up the brace (`gripShiftR`, `aimSide`). Both paws keep this hold at rest (`restAim`).
- **The left arm (1.1).** The left elbow points down and forward (`reachL`, `poleL`).
- **The head (1.1).** The head stays square and upright at the mouthpiece (`headTurn`).
- **The bell (1.1).** The bell section lies beside the slide, at its left.
- **The positions (1.2, 1.3).** Each note takes the lowest position on the partials of B♭1, with
  no seventh partial (`slidePosition()`, `PARTIALS`). The steps keep the ratio of the real
  distances (`slideTravel()`).
- **The slide move (1.5).** The slide moves late and fast, ends at the note start, and stops in
  each position (`WIND_MOTION.slideSec`). The speed grows with the distance.
- **The rest (1.7).** The slide stays closed.
- **The seated posture (1.8).** The panda sits upright, off the chair back, with the thighs down
  and the soles flat (design 5.2).

The app does not follow these facts:

- **The slide angle (1.1, 1.4).** The slide points a little below the level and to the right
  (`PANDA_PLAY`). Straight ahead, the short arm of the panda reaches almost none of the slide
  travel.
- **The slide reach (1.2).** The slide travel is a small part of the real travel, with the right
  arm straight at the seventh position (`slideReachOf()`). The paw at the far side of the brace
  keeps the palm off the audience, and costs a part of the reach.
- **The alternate positions (1.3).** The app has none.
- **The arm (1.4).** The two-bone IK moves the elbow most, with no lead of the wrist (`armRig()`).
- **The rest place (1.7).** No source gives it. The slide hangs down in front, with both paws on
  their grips (`rest`, `restShift` of `WIND_HOLDS.trombone`, design 5.2).

### 4.2 The other winds

The app follows these facts:

- **The flute (2.4, `FOX_FLUTE`).** The head turns to the left, and the shoulders stay square and
  low. The flute lies about square to the face and slopes down to the right (`FOX_FLUTE_BACK`,
  `play`). The left paw holds the flute near the lip plate from its front, and the right paw lies
  behind the flute and a little below it, so that the fingers of both paws curl over the top onto
  the keys (`aimSide`, `gripCurl`). At rest, the flute stands on the right thigh in the right paw
  (`rest`, `restShift`, `gripAround`).
- **The oboe (2.3, `MOUSE_OBOE`).** The oboe stands at about 40° from the body (`hanging()` of
  `src/models/instruments/woodwinds.ts`, `play`). The reed is in the mouth under the long muzzle
  (`lipShift`), and the head stays up. The left paw holds the upper joint, and the right paw the
  lower joint (`gripShiftL`, `gripShiftR`). The upper arms hang below the shoulders, with the
  elbows a little out (`poleL`, `poleR`).
- **The clarinet (3, `WOLF_CLARINET`).** The clarinet is at the middle of the body, and the head
  does not tilt toward it (`lipShift`). The elbows point down and a little out. At rest, the
  clarinet stands upright between the knees, with both paws on their grips (`rest`, `restShift`).
- **The woodwind palms (2.3, 2.4, 3).** Each paw of the oboe, the clarinet, and the bassoon holds
  its tube from the side of its own shoulder. The palm faces the tube, the fingers curl over the
  front in a C, and the thumb points up the tube (`faceTube`, `aimAcross`, `aimSide`).
- **The bassoon (3, `BEAVER_BASSOON`).** The bassoon lies diagonal from the right hip up past the
  left shoulder, with the bell above the head (`BEAVER_PLAY`, `bassoonParts()`). The left paw is
  high on the wing joint, and the right paw low on the boot joint. The bocal is near the level at
  the reed, and the reed is under the front teeth (`lipShift`).
- **The horn (2.2, `PIG_HORN`).** The left paw presses the valves at the middle of the body
  (`fingering`). The horn lies across the body, with the bell at the right of the torso and in
  front of it, off the leg, and the mouthpiece slopes down (`HORN_TURN`, `PIG_TURNS`). The valves
  follow the single F horn chart (`BRASS_FUNDAMENTALS.horn`, `HORN_PARTIALS`).
- **The trumpet (2.1, `MONKEY_TRUMPET`).** The right paw presses the valves, and the left paw
  holds the trumpet around the upright casings (`fingering`, `faceTube`). The right palm lies
  above the valves and faces down. The trumpet points a little down, straight ahead (`trumpet()`
  of `src/models/instruments/brass.ts`, `play`). The valves follow the trumpet fingering
  (`VALVES`, `semitonesBelowPartial()`).
- **The tuba (3, `RHINO_TUBA`).** The rhino stands, and the tuba stands against its body, so the
  paws carry no weight (`INSTRUMENT_SUPPORTS.tuba` of `src/models/player.ts`). The right palm
  faces the valve tops, and the left paw grips the main branch at its left side, with the back of
  the paw out to the left. Both elbows point down and out, away from the rib cage. The bell stands
  at the left of the head (`play`).

The app does not follow these facts:

- **The flute body (2.4).** The chest faces the front. The head does not tilt to the right ear.
- **The flute rest (2.4).** The right paw holds the flute higher than the right-hand keys, and the
  flute leans a little. Upright on the thigh, the right arm goes into the body.
- **The oboe size (2.3).** The mouse plays a smaller oboe (`SpeciesParams.oboeScale`), in place of
  longer arms. No source gives the size of a smaller oboe.
- **The oboe hands (2.3).** The paws reach forward at the height of the shoulders, because the big
  head of the mouse puts the reed high above the shoulder joints.
- **The oboe rest (2.3).** The mouse stands, and its round belly covers the thighs. Thus the oboe
  stands upright in front of the belly, with both paws on their grips (`rest`, `restShift`).
- **The oboe breath (2.3).** The app has no breath out of the stale air.
- **The clarinet angle (3).** The clarinet hangs nearer to the vertical than 45° (`hanging()`,
  `play`), as the wolf of `orchestra-latest.png` and `orchestra-side.png` holds it. The long muzzle
  puts the mouthpiece far in front of the chest, so at 45° the bell is far in front of the knees.
- **The clarinet rest (3).** The bell rests on nothing. The round belly reaches the seat front, so
  an upright clarinet has no room on the seat, and on the right knee the left forearm goes into
  the belly.
- **The bassoon (3).** The app has no strap. The beaver plays a smaller bassoon
  (`SpeciesParams.bassoonScale`), so that it keeps its arms. No source gives the elbows: the
  elbows of `poleL` and `poleR` give the smallest twist of the left forearm. No source gives the
  rest: the bassoon moves off the mouth, with both paws on their grips (`BEAVER_REST`).
- **The right hand in the horn (2.2).** The right paw stays on the axis of the bell, inside its
  opening (`HORN_PALM_DEPTH`), in place of against the bell wall at 2 o'clock. A deeper paw puts
  the wrist through the bell wall.
- **The horn elbows (2.2).** The left elbow points out and forward (`reachL`, `poleL`). The long
  snout puts the valves far in front of the chest, and with the elbow down and in, the left
  forearm goes into the chest.
- **The horn rest (2.2).** The round belly covers the short thighs, so the horn rests on the belly
  over the lap, with the bell at the right and the leadpipe up (`rest`, `restShift`).
- **The double horn (2.2).** The app has no B♭ side.
- **The trumpet thumb (2.1).** The right thumb lies above the lead pipe.
- **The trumpet elbows (2.1).** Both elbows point down and out, and the forearms rise steeply. The
  big head of the monkey puts the mouthpiece high above the shoulder joints. With the elbows out
  and level, the right upper arm goes flat.
- **The trumpet size and rest (2.1).** The monkey plays a smaller trumpet
  (`SpeciesParams.trumpetScale`). No source gives its size or the rest. At rest, the trumpet turns
  down in front of the chest, and both paws keep the hold of the play (`restAim`,
  `restGripShiftL`).
- **The tuba (3).** The app draws no strap. The lead pipe is longer (`SpeciesParams.tubaPipe`),
  because the long muzzle puts the mouthpiece far in front of the shoulder joints. No source gives
  the length of a lead pipe or the rest. At rest, the tuba moves off the mouth, with both paws on
  their grips (`rest`).
